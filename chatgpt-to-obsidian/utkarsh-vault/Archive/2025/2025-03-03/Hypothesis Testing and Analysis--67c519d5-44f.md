### **Testing of Hypothesis, Non-Parametric Methods, and Sequential Analysis** 

## **Introduction** 
Hypothesis testing is a fundamental concept in statistics used to make inferences about populations based on sample data. It helps determine whether observed data significantly deviates from an assumed condition or if variations are due to random chance. 

Non-parametric methods are statistical techniques that do not rely on specific probability distributions, making them useful when data does not meet normality assumptions. 

Sequential analysis is a statistical method where data is analyzed as it is collected, allowing for early decision-making instead of waiting for a fixed sample size. 

---

## **Statistical Hypothesis** 
A **statistical hypothesis** is an assumption or statement about a population parameter that can be tested using statistical techniques. It is classified into: 

1. **Simple Hypothesis**: Specifies the population distribution completely. 
2. **Composite Hypothesis**: Does not specify the population distribution completely (e.g., only states a range of values). 

---

## **Test of a Statistical Hypothesis** 
A hypothesis test follows a structured procedure to determine if the assumption about the population is valid or should be rejected. 

### **Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\))** 
The null hypothesis is the default assumption that there is no effect or difference in the population. It represents a statement of "no change" or "no relationship." 

Example: 
\(H_0: \mu = 50\) (The population mean is 50). 

### **Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_1\))** 
The alternative hypothesis contradicts \(H_0\) and represents the claim that there is a significant effect or difference. 

Example: 
\(H_1: \mu \neq 50\) (The population mean is not 50). 

---

## **Critical Region** 
The **critical region** is the range of values where \(H_0\) is rejected. It is determined by the chosen significance level. If the test statistic falls within this region, the null hypothesis is rejected. 

---

## **Two Types of Errors** 
1. **Type I Error (\(\alpha\))**: Rejecting \(H_0\) when it is actually true (False Positive). 
2. **Type II Error (\(\beta\))**: Failing to reject \(H_0\) when it is actually false (False Negative). 

The probability of making a Type I error is called the **level of significance (\(\alpha\))**. 

---

## **Level of Significance (\(\alpha\))** 
The significance level (\(\alpha\)) is the probability of committing a Type I error. Common values are **0.05 (5%)** and **0.01 (1%)**, meaning we allow a 5% or 1% chance of rejecting a true null hypothesis. 

---

## **Power of the Test** 
The power of a test is the probability of correctly rejecting \(H_0\) when it is false. It is given by: 

\\[
Power = 1 - \beta
\\] 

A **higher power** means a test is more effective at detecting true effects. Power increases with a larger sample size and a higher significance level. 

---

This forms the foundation of hypothesis testing, which is widely used in research, industry, and decision-making processes.
