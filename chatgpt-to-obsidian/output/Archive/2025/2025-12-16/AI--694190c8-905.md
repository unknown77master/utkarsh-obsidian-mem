## **Q7**

### **a) Short note on Circumscription. \[5\]**

Circumscription is a form of **non-monotonic reasoning** used in Artificial Intelligence to handle situations involving incomplete information. It was introduced by John McCarthy to formalize **common-sense reasoning**.

The main idea of circumscription is to **minimize the extension of certain predicates**, thereby assuming that what is not known to be true is false. This allows the system to make default assumptions unless contradicted by explicit information.

Circumscription is widely used to represent assumptions such as:
- Objects have no properties except those specified.
- Actions have no effects except those stated.

Although powerful, circumscription is computationally expensive and difficult to apply in large-scale knowledge bases.

---

### **b) Theory of Beliefs. \[6\]**

The theory of beliefs deals with representing and reasoning about an agent’s **beliefs rather than absolute facts**. It is used when knowledge is uncertain, incomplete, or subjective.

Key aspects include:
- Beliefs may be **true or false**, unlike facts.
- Beliefs can change over time due to new information.
- Multiple agents may hold **different beliefs** about the same situation.

Formal models such as **belief revision theory**, **modal logic**, and **Dempster–Shafer theory** are used to manage belief systems. The theory of beliefs is essential in applications like expert systems, multi-agent systems, and decision-making under uncertainty.

---

### **c) Ontology of Situation Calculus. \[6\]**

The ontology of situation calculus defines the **basic entities and relationships** used to model dynamic systems.

Its main components are:
- **Actions**: Events that cause changes.
- **Situations**: Histories or sequences of actions.
- **Fluents**: Properties whose values may change over situations.

Situation calculus provides a logical framework to describe how actions transform the world from one situation to another. The ontology supports reasoning about action effects, preconditions, and persistence, making it useful in planning and robotics.

## **Q8**

### **a) Short note on AI Architecture. \[5\]**

AI architecture refers to the **structural design of an artificial intelligence system**, defining how its components interact to perform intelligent behavior.

A typical AI architecture includes:
- **Perception module** to sense the environment
- **Knowledge base** for storing information
- **Reasoning or inference engine**
- **Learning module**
- **Action or execution module**

The objective of AI architecture is to ensure scalability, modularity, and effective decision-making. Examples include reactive architectures, deliberative architectures, and hybrid architectures.

---

### **b) Planning Approaches: Discussion and Analysis. \[6\]**

Planning in AI involves generating a sequence of actions to achieve a goal. Major planning approaches include:

1. **State-space Planning** 
 - Explores possible states from initial to goal state.
 - Can be forward or backward planning.

2. **Plan-space Planning** 
 - Focuses on refining partial plans.
 - Supports flexibility and parallel actions.

3. **Hierarchical Planning (HTN)** 
 - Decomposes complex tasks into simpler subtasks.
 - Efficient for large problems.

4. **Probabilistic Planning** 
 - Handles uncertainty in action outcomes.

Each approach has trade-offs in terms of complexity, flexibility, and computational cost.

---

### **c) Time and Schedule in Temporal Planning. \[6\]**

In temporal planning, **time** refers to the explicit representation of action durations and temporal constraints. Actions are not instantaneous and may overlap.

A **schedule** is the allocation of actions along a timeline such that:
- Temporal constraints are satisfied.
- Resource conflicts are avoided.
- Goals are achieved efficiently.

Temporal planning ensures that actions are executed in the correct order and at appropriate times. It is crucial in real-world applications such as robotics, logistics, and project management.

### **Classical Planning**

Classical planning is a traditional planning approach in Artificial Intelligence in which the environment is assumed to be **fully observable, deterministic, static, and discrete**. The planner has complete knowledge of the initial state, available actions, and the goal state.

In classical planning, actions are defined using **preconditions and effects**. A plan is generated as a **sequence of actions** that transforms the initial state into a goal state. Common representations used in classical planning include **STRIPS** and **PDDL**.

Classical planning typically searches in the **state space**, either forward from the initial state or backward from the goal state. While this approach is conceptually simple and well-suited for well-defined problems, it suffers from **state-space explosion** when applied to large or complex domains.

---

### **Hierarchical Planning**

Hierarchical planning, also known as **Hierarchical Task Network (HTN) planning**, addresses the complexity of planning by structuring problems at multiple levels of abstraction.

In hierarchical planning, a complex task is first expressed as a **high-level goal** and then recursively **decomposed into smaller subtasks** using predefined methods. These subtasks are further decomposed until they reach **primitive actions** that can be directly executed.

The key advantages of hierarchical planning are:
- Reduced search space
- Improved scalability
- Better representation of domain knowledge

Hierarchical planning is widely used in real-world applications such as **robotics, automated manufacturing, and workflow management**, where tasks naturally follow structured procedures.

---

**Conclusion:** 
Classical planning focuses on generating action sequences in a flat state space, whereas hierarchical planning introduces abstraction and task decomposition to manage complexity efficiently.

## **Q5**

### **a) Non-Monotonic Reasoning and Logics Associated with It. \[9\]**

Non-monotonic reasoning is a form of reasoning in Artificial Intelligence in which the set of conclusions **does not monotonically increase** with the addition of new knowledge. That is, previously derived conclusions **may be withdrawn** when new information becomes available. This type of reasoning closely resembles **human commonsense reasoning**, where assumptions are revised when exceptions are observed.

In contrast to classical (monotonic) logic, non-monotonic reasoning allows systems to operate under **incomplete or uncertain knowledge**.

#### **Need for Non-Monotonic Reasoning**
- Real-world knowledge is often incomplete.
- Assumptions are made in the absence of full information.
- New facts may invalidate earlier conclusions.

#### **Logics Associated with Non-Monotonic Reasoning**

1. **Default Logic** 
 Introduced by Reiter, default logic allows rules with default assumptions. A conclusion is drawn unless there is evidence to the contrary. 
 *Example:* Birds normally fly, unless specified otherwise.

2. **Circumscription** 
 Proposed by McCarthy, circumscription minimizes the extension of predicates to assume that what is not known to be true is false. It is used to formalize common-sense assumptions.

3. **Autoepistemic Logic** 
 This logic models reasoning about an agent’s own beliefs. It allows statements about what the agent knows or does not know.

4. **Defeasible Logic** 
 Supports rules that can be overridden by more specific or stronger rules. It is computationally efficient and used in legal reasoning.

5. **Truth Maintenance Systems (TMS)** 
 Maintains consistency by tracking dependencies between beliefs and revising conclusions when assumptions change.

Thus, non-monotonic reasoning provides a flexible framework for intelligent systems dealing with dynamic and uncertain environments.

---

### **b) Unification Algorithm in Predicate Logic with Example. \[9\]**

Unification is a fundamental process in predicate logic that determines whether two logical expressions can be made identical by substituting variables with constants or other variables. It is essential for reasoning, resolution, and inference in first-order logic.

#### **Unification Algorithm Steps**

1. Compare the two predicates to be unified.
2. Check whether predicate names and arity are the same.
3. Match corresponding arguments one by one.
4. If variables are present, substitute them consistently.
5. Ensure no variable is substituted with an expression containing itself.
6. Continue until all terms match or failure occurs.

The result of successful unification is the **Most General Unifier (MGU)**.

#### **Example**

Consider the predicates:

\\[
P(x, y) \quad \text{and} \quad P(John, z)
\\]

**Step-by-step unification:**
- Match predicate symbols: same → proceed
- Compare first arguments: 
 \(x = John\)
- Compare second arguments: 
 \(y = z\)

**Substitution Set (MGU):**
\\[
\{x / John, y / z\}
\\]

After substitution, both predicates become identical.

#### **Importance of Unification**
- Enables application of inference rules
- Supports resolution-based theorem proving
- Reduces search space in logical reasoning

---

### **Conclusion**

Non-monotonic reasoning allows intelligent systems to revise conclusions dynamically, while the unification algorithm provides the operational foundation for reasoning in predicate logic.

### **Min–Max (Minimax) Search Procedure**

The **Min–Max search procedure**, also known as the **Minimax algorithm**, is a fundamental decision-making technique used in **game playing Artificial Intelligence**. It is applied in **two-player, zero-sum games** where one player’s gain is exactly equal to the other player’s loss. Typical examples include chess, tic-tac-toe, and checkers.

The objective of the Min–Max procedure is to determine the **optimal move** for a player by assuming that the opponent will also play optimally.

---

### **Basic Concept**

- **MAX player**: Tries to maximize the utility (score).
- **MIN player**: Tries to minimize the utility.

The game is represented as a **game tree**, where:
- Nodes represent game states.
- Edges represent possible moves.
- Leaf nodes have utility values.

---

### **Min–Max Algorithm Steps**

1. Generate the complete game tree from the current state.
2. Assign utility values to terminal (leaf) nodes.
3. Propagate values upward:
 - At **MAX nodes**, choose the maximum value among children.
 - At **MIN nodes**, choose the minimum value among children.
4. The root node’s value determines the optimal move for MAX.

---

### **Example**

Consider a simple game tree:

```
 MAX
 / \
 MIN MIN
 / \ / \
 3 5 2 9
```

**Step 1:** 
At MIN nodes:
- Left MIN chooses `min(3, 5) = 3`
- Right MIN chooses `min(2, 9) = 2`

**Step 2:** 
At MAX node:
- MAX chooses `max(3, 2) = 3`

---

### **Result**

The optimal move for the MAX player leads to a utility value of **3**. Hence, MAX will select the **left subtree**.

---

### **Advantages**
- Guarantees optimal decision assuming optimal opponent.
- Simple and systematic approach.

### **Limitations**
- Computationally expensive for large game trees.
- Performance decreases with increased depth.

---

### **Conclusion**

The Min–Max search procedure provides a rational framework for decision-making in competitive environments. Despite its limitations, it forms the foundation for advanced algorithms such as **Alpha–Beta pruning**.
