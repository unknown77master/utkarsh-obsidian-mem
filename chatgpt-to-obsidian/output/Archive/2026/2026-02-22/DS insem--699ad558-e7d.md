### **Q. Describe Bayes’ Theorem with example. (10 Marks)** 

### **Answer:** 

### **Introduction**

entity\["book","Bayes' Theorem","probability theory concept"\] is a fundamental concept in probability theory and statistics. It is used to determine the conditional probability of an event based on prior knowledge of related events. It plays an important role in statistical inference and decision-making.

---

### **Statement of Bayes’ Theorem**

Bayes’ Theorem states that:

\\[
P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)}
\\]

---

### **Explanation of Terms**

- \( P(A|B) \) = Posterior probability (probability of event A given B has occurred) 
- \( P(B|A) \) = Likelihood (probability of event B given A has occurred) 
- \( P(A) \) = Prior probability of event A 
- \( P(B) \) = Total probability of event B 

---

### **Need of Bayes’ Theorem**

- To update probabilities when new evidence is available 
- Used in medical diagnosis 
- Used in spam email filtering 
- Used in machine learning and artificial intelligence 

---

### **Example**

Suppose a disease affects 1% of a population.

Let: 
- \( P(D) = 0.01 \) (Probability that a person has disease) 
- \( P(\overline{D}) = 0.99 \) 
- Test is 95% accurate for diseased persons 
 \( P(Pos|D) = 0.95 \) 
- Test gives false positive in 5% cases 
 \( P(Pos|\overline{D}) = 0.05 \) 

We need to find: 
**Probability that a person actually has the disease given that the test result is positive**, i.e., \( P(D|Pos) \)

---

### **Step 1: Find Total Probability of Positive Test**

\\[
P(Pos) = P(Pos|D)P(D) + P(Pos|\overline{D})P(\overline{D})
\\]

\\[
P(Pos) = (0.95 \times 0.01) + (0.05 \times 0.99)
\\]

\\[
P(Pos) = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059
\\]

---

### **Step 2: Apply Bayes’ Theorem**

\\[
P(D|Pos) = \frac{P(Pos|D)P(D)}{P(Pos)}
\\]

\\[
P(D|Pos) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.01}{0.059}
\\]

\\[
P(D|Pos) = \frac{0.0095}{0.059}
\\]

\\[
P(D|Pos) \approx 0.161
\\]

---

### **Final Answer**

The probability that a person actually has the disease given that the test result is positive is **16.1%**.

This shows that even if the test is accurate, the actual probability depends on the prior probability of the disease.

---

### **Conclusion**

Thus, Bayes’ Theorem helps in updating prior probability using new evidence to obtain posterior probability. It is widely used in medical diagnosis, artificial intelligence, and statistical inference.

### **Q. Explain Null Hypothesis and Alternative Hypothesis by considering an example of flipping a coin.**

### **Answer:**

In statistics, a **hypothesis** is an assumption or statement about a population parameter that can be tested using sample data. In hypothesis testing, two types of hypotheses are used: **Null Hypothesis** and **Alternative Hypothesis**.

---

### **1. Null Hypothesis (H₀)**

The **Null Hypothesis** is the default assumption that there is **no significant difference or effect**. It assumes that the observed result occurs purely by chance.

**Example (Coin Toss):**

Suppose we want to test whether a coin is fair.

A fair coin has an equal probability of heads and tails.

Therefore, the null hypothesis is:

**H₀: The coin is fair.** 
\\[
P(\text{Heads}) = 0.5
\\]

This means the probability of getting heads is **50%**.

---

### **2. Alternative Hypothesis (H₁ or Hₐ)**

The **Alternative Hypothesis** is the statement that contradicts the null hypothesis. It suggests that there **is a significant difference or effect**.

**Example (Coin Toss):**

If we suspect that the coin is biased, the alternative hypothesis is:

**H₁: The coin is not fair.** 
\\[
P(\text{Heads}) \ne 0.5
\\]

This means the probability of getting heads is **not equal to 50%**.

---

### **Illustration**

Suppose we toss the coin **100 times** and obtain:

- Heads = 70 
- Tails = 30 

Since the result deviates significantly from the expected **50 heads and 50 tails**, we may reject the null hypothesis and accept the alternative hypothesis that the coin may be biased.

---

### **Conclusion**

Thus, the **Null Hypothesis assumes no effect or no difference**, while the **Alternative Hypothesis suggests that a difference exists**. In the coin flipping example, the null hypothesis states that the coin is fair, while the alternative hypothesis states that the coin is biased.

### **Q. Explain different measures of dispersion.**

### **Answer:**

**Measures of Dispersion** are statistical methods used to describe the spread or variability of data values in a dataset. While measures of central tendency (mean, median, mode) show the central value, measures of dispersion show how far the data values are spread from the central value.

---

## **Types of Measures of Dispersion**

### **1. Range**

Range is the simplest measure of dispersion. It is the difference between the highest value and the lowest value in a dataset.

**Formula:**

\\[
\text{Range} = \text{Maximum Value} - \text{Minimum Value}
\\]

**Example:**

Data: 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 

Maximum value = 30 
Minimum value = 10 

Range = 30 − 10 = **20**

---

### **2. Mean Deviation**

Mean deviation is the average of the absolute differences between each data value and the mean of the dataset.

**Formula:**

\\[
MD = \frac{\sum |x - \bar{x}|}{n}
\\]

Where: 
\(x\) = individual value 
\(\bar{x}\) = mean 
\(n\) = number of observations

**Example:** 
If mean marks of students is 70, mean deviation shows how far individual marks differ from 70.

---

### **3. Variance**

Variance measures the average of the squared differences between each value and the mean.

**Formula:**

\\[
\sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}{n}
\\]

It shows how much the data values vary from the mean.

**Example:** 
If marks of students vary widely from the mean, variance will be large.

---

### **4. Standard Deviation**

Standard deviation is the square root of variance. It is one of the most commonly used measures of dispersion.

**Formula:**

\\[
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}{n}}
\\]

It shows how much the values deviate from the mean on average.

**Example:** 
If the standard deviation is small, the data values are close to the mean. If it is large, the data values are widely spread.

---

### **Conclusion**

Thus, measures of dispersion such as **range, mean deviation, variance, and standard deviation** help in understanding the variability of data. They provide information about how data values are distributed around the central value.

# **Q4 (b) What does the Pearson Correlation Coefficient Test explain?**

The **Pearson Correlation Coefficient** measures the **strength and direction of the linear relationship between two variables**.

It is represented by **r**.

**Formula**

\\[
r = \frac{Cov(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}
\\]

Where 
X and Y = variables 
σ = standard deviation

---

### **Range of Pearson Correlation**

| Value of r | Meaning |
|---|---|
| +1 | Perfect positive correlation |
| 0 | No correlation |
| -1 | Perfect negative correlation |

---

Suppose we compare:

| Study Hours | Marks |
|---|---|
| 2 | 40 |
| 4 | 50 |
| 6 | 65 |
| 8 | 80 |

As study hours increase, marks also increase. 
This indicates a **positive correlation**.

# **Q4 (c) Explain Type I and Type II Errors**

In **Hypothesis Testing**, two types of errors may occur.

---

## **Type I Error**

Type I error occurs when **a true null hypothesis is rejected**.

It is also called a **false positive**.

**Example**

Null Hypothesis: A person is healthy. 
Test result says the person has a disease.

Here the null hypothesis was true but rejected.

---

## **Type II Error**

Type II error occurs when **a false null hypothesis is accepted**.

It is also called a **false negative**.

**Example**

Null Hypothesis: A person is healthy. 
Test result says the person is healthy, but actually the person has a disease.

---

### **Summary**

| Error Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Type I Error | Rejecting a true null hypothesis |
| Type II Error | Accepting a false null hypothesis |
